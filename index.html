Porchlight

Catches a scam before your family does

Nothing you paste ever leaves this browser. Porchlight checks messages using two things running locally: a set of hand-written scam rules, and a small statistical model trained right here on your device when this page loaded.

Rules catch known patterns — urgent deadlines, gift-card demands, requests for a social security number, a claim that your account or verification is suspended paired with a link to "fix" it, or a dangled overpayment/refund paired with a link to "claim" it. The model catches phrasing the rules miss. If either finds a serious red flag (like a money request paired with a threat), the result is marked risky no matter what the other one says.

When a message claims to be from a real company, Porchlight also looks up that company's actual verified phone number and website, so you never have to trust the contact info printed in a suspicious message.

When a message claims to be from someone in your saved contacts, Porchlight checks whether it actually came from that person's saved number, and whether they have ever asked you for money before. A saved contact asking for money for the first time, or a message claiming to be them from a different number, is treated as a possible compromised phone or account, not a safe message — even if nothing else about the wording looks suspicious.

To stop a scammer from "warming up" a contact's history with a small, harmless-looking ask first, a flagged money request only becomes part of that person's normal history after you or a caregiver explicitly confirm it was legitimate. Porchlight never assumes a risky message was fine just because it went unanswered.

Use the country/region picker to switch which real tax agencies, banks, postal services, and fraud-reporting hotlines Porchlight checks against, and it also recognizes £, €, and ₹ amounts and scam patterns common outside the US, such as customs-fee package scams and authority-impersonation scams like India's "digital arrest" scam. The message text itself still needs to be in English for the on-device model to read it — detecting scam messages written in other languages is a planned next step, not something this version does.

Money requests are also weighed against the relationship you've tagged for a sender. A first-ever ask is always scrutinized no matter how close the relationship is — closeness doesn't get to skip that check. But relationship changes what happens next: a spouse who's asked for money before barely registers, while a "lover" or new online contact who keeps asking still gets a light ongoing flag, since that pattern — small, repeated, trust-building asks — is exactly how romance and grooming scams operate. The same wording from a stranger claiming a romantic or new relationship is flagged even on the very first message, since content alone genuinely can't tell a scammer's affectionate language apart from a real partner's — real couples use the same words. Marking a contact "allowed to ask for money" is respected for low-risk relationships, but for high-risk ones it downgrades the flag rather than silencing it entirely, since the person most likely to grant that permission is often the one being actively groomed.

Porchlight also recognizes UPI payment and "collect" requests and QR-code pay prompts. Approving a UPI collect request moves money out of your account even though it can look like you're the one receiving something — only approve one if you already know exactly who sent it and why.

Porchlight is English-only for now, on purpose — the detection features are still being actively worked on, and adding other languages back in is a deliberate later step rather than something done alongside every change.

This runs the full rule-and-model pipeline against 88 messages that were not used to train the model, and reports how it did. 60 are edge cases I wrote by hand to probe specific behaviors — including 22 built specifically to stress-test the family-emergency scam rule against different relationships, injuries, and payment methods, plus close benign look-alikes that should not trigger it; 28 are real messages from the public SMS Spam Collection dataset (including its "smishing" category), held out from the training data added below. Treat the numbers as a sanity check on the approach, not a scientific accuracy claim.

"Flagged" means the message came back as Caution or Danger; "Not flagged" means Safe. Precision is: of the messages Porchlight flagged, how many really were scams. Recall is: of the real scams in the set, how many Porchlight caught.
